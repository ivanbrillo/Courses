Value through Services

Services are different form products

  • Service-Dominant Logic (S-D Logic) views service—the application of skills and knowledge for the benefit of others—as the core of all economic exchange, not goods or money.
  • Although services are central, this reality is often hidden behind tangible goods, money, or institutional structures. In fact, goods are just vehicles for delivering services—their true value lies in the service they enable.
  • Competitive advantage comes from intangible resources, particularly knowledge and skills, rather than physical assets.
  • As a result, every economy is fundamentally a service economy, even when it appears to be product- or manufacturing-based.
  • Crucially, customers play an active role in creating value through how they use and interpret the service. Companies can only provide value propositions, not guarantee value itself.
  • This approach is inherently customer-focused and relational, emphasizing interaction and responsiveness to individual needs. Value is subjective and context-specific, determined uniquely by the beneficiary, not by the producer.

Service Characteristics

Intangibility

Services cannot be seen, touched, or stored like physical products

  • A service is an action or activity performed by someone (e.g., a haircut, a legal consultation), not a physical object. It's something you experience, not something you own.

  • difficulty in evaluation → no physical inspection
  • To overcome intangibility, service providers often use physical symbols (like uniforms, clean facilities, certificates) to reassure customers and convey professionalism or quality
  • Value comes from use, not possession → benefits of non-ownership

Inseparability

services are produced and consumed at the same time

  • Since the provider is often directly involved in delivering the service, their behavior, attitude, and skill have a direct impact on the customer's experience
  • Because staff are key to the service delivery, careful hiring, continuous training, and performance-based rewards are crucial to maintaining service quality and customer satisfaction
  • In many services (like restaurants, flights, or gyms), customers share the service environment. Ensuring a pleasant shared experience requires managing interactions and minimizing conflict between customers

Perishability

Perishability refers to the idea that services cannot be stored, saved, or inventoried for future use

  • A service (like an empty airline seat or an unbooked hotel room for the night) is lost forever if not used when available. It can't be "stocked" for future use.
  • Hiring flexible or part-time employees helps service providers scale their workforce up or down based on demand, avoiding overstaffing during slow periods.
  • Training staff to perform multiple roles helps manage unexpected demand surges and makes better use of human resources during quiet times.
  • Encouraging customers to engage in self-service (e.g. self-check-in kiosks or online bookings) helps improve efficiency and manage peak demand periods.
  • Charging different prices at different times (like off-peak discounts or weekend surcharges) helps shift demand to less busy periods.
  • Promotions, deals, or loyalty incentives during low-demand times help reduce the negative effects of unused service capacity.
  • Booking systems allow providers to control and plan for demand in advance, reducing unpredictability and helping avoid over- or under-utilization.

Variability

  • refers to the fact that service quality can vary greatly from one provider to another
  • Unlike goods, which can be mass-produced with consistent quality, services often vary because they're delivered in real-time by humans. For example, two waiters at the same restaurant may provide very different customer experiences.
  • Monitoring performance (through feedback forms, reviews, or quality checks) helps ensure service standards are being met and allows for correction when variability arises.
  • While people deliver services, equipment (e.g., diagnostic tools in healthcare, machines in laundromats) can support consistency and reduce human error, helping stabilize service quality

The services marketing mix is an extension of the 4P’s:

  • Physical evidence → Customers look for clues to examine the likely quality of a service by inspecting the tangible evidence
  • People → to form them, their behaviour form the experience for the customer
  • Process → invest in IT

Relationship Marketing

Six Benefits of Strong Customer Relationships (eg. subscription, or long term contract):

  1. Increased purchases;
  1. Lower costs;
  1. Lifetime value (eg from a subscription)
  1. Sustainable competitive advantage → supplier switching cost (eg. from a software A to B)
  1. Word of mouth promotion;
  1. Decreased staff turnover

Recurrent value is much more precious than non recurrent → otherwise high marketing work to sell new serices

Meeting Customer Expectations

  1. Reliability: is the service consistent and dependable?
  1. Assurance: that customers can trust the service company and its staff.
  1. Responsiveness: how quickly do service staff respond to customer problems, requests and questions?
  1. Empathy: that service staff act in a friendly and polite manner and care for their customers.
  1. Tangibles: how well-managed is the tangible evidence of the service (e.g. staff appearance, decor, layout)?

Social Marketing

Social Marketing refers to the use of marketing principles and techniques to influence behaviors that benefit individuals and communities for the greater social good, rather than for commercial profit. It is often used by governments, NGOs, public health organizations, and non-profits to tackle social issues.
