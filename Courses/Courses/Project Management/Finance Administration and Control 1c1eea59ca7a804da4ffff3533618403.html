a) Owner Capital (Equity)

Funds contributed by the owner, family, or friends (often referred to as the "3Fs": fool, family, friends). These funds are typically collected informally.

Divided in:

  • Common Stock (Initial Funds): Divided into shares
  • Profit Reserves: Profits not distributed as dividends
  • Current Net Profit: May be distributed as dividends later

  • Usually provides a relatively small amount, primarily for starting the business
  • In a Personal Company: Owner needs to repay debts even in bankruptcy
  • In a Limited Company: Partners are responsible only for the amount of their shares
    • SRL (Limited Responsibility Company) → Shareholders' liability is limited to their capital contribution
      • Shares cannot be offered to the public or listed on stock exchanges
      • Smaller than SPA
    • SPA (Limited Company) → Shareholders have limited liability (only risk their investment)

b) Debt Capital

Expands the owner's funds by allowing other people to share in financing the company.

Divides in:

  1. Short-term (Current Liabilities): Less than 1 year
  1. Long-term Loans: For fixed assets (e.g., plants)
    • Typically repaid over the lifetime of the asset
    • Banks rarely provide loans with expiration beyond 20 years

Debt characteristic:

  1. Interest: Calculated as a percentage of the nominal value, paid annually regardless of company performance
    • Paid before calculating profits for shareholders
    • Interest can be fixed or variable over the years
  1. Capital Repayment: The principal must be repaid by the expiration date
    • If the company can't repay, it goes into default
    • In practice, final capital repayments are often spread out yearly by increasing the interest

Appendix: Common Stock

Common stock (capitale sociale) represents the total amount of money provided by shareholders.

Capital Increase

Companies can request additional capital from shareholders

  • If existing shareholders can't provide more capital, new shareholders can be brought in (requires shareholder assembly approval) → dilution
    • New shareholders dilute the ownership of existing ones
  • By law, new stock must be offered proportionally to existing shareholders if approved by the assembly

Shares

The common stock is divided into a number of shares, each with a nominal value

Shares provide legal rights:

  1. Voting Rights: Typically one vote per share, with decisions made by majority
    • The assembly approves the balance sheet (assets and liabilities)
    • If not approved, the CEO typically resigns
    • The assembly elects the Board (with a President), which then elects the CEO
  1. Profit Rights: Shareholders are entitled to profits for company performance.
    • Gross profit π=TR−TC\pi = TR-TCπ=TR−TC﻿
    • It is taxed, after it becomes Net profit
    • Net profit can be used for:
      1. Reserves: Liquidity for further investments (self-funding)
        • Not taking profits now can increase share price in the market
        • Alternative to bank loans for investments
      1. Dividends: Distributed based on number of shares held

Share Valuation

Shares trade on stock markets at their market price, not nominal value

  • Current price reflects expected future profits → example NVIDIA or TESLA (drop of buys)
  • Market dynamics follow game theory principles:
    • People sell when they expect profits to decrease
    • Timing is critical to avoid losses

Economic cycle vs financial cycle

  1. The economic cycle starts with the production and ends with the sales of the products.
  1. The financial cycle starts with the payment for the costs needed to produce and ends with the reception of payment from sales.

The two cycles are not synchronized.

One of the major difficulties in general management, and particularly in the startup phase, is the smooth coordination between the economic and the financial cycles.

  • startup operate in loss for years, paying salaries and accounts, but not selling anything

Economic equilibrium

  • In each year it must be that total revenues > total cost

It may happen that there are temporary losses. An economic disequilibrium can be sustained only occasionally and for few years in line

  • Losses can be absorbed by decreasing the Issued common stock, but not beyond a given threshold defined by the legislation. In case of losses that exceed this threshold the company is liquidated

After economic disequilibrium (losses) firms may experience increasing difficulties in meeting the deadlines for cash payments and enter into liquidity crisis and insolvency

  • Insolvency may lead the firm to the bankrupcy
  • you need economic equilibrium to keep financial equilibrium (otherwise insolvency)

Financial equilibrium

In each year it must be that:

  • fixed assets are covered by owners’ funds and long term loans
  • current liabilities are covered by current assets
  • short term cash needs are fully covered

A firm may be profitable but in financial disequilibrium if

  • credits are subject to high risk of insolvency and
  • terms of payment of credits largely exceed terms of payment of debts
  • inventories are not sold regularly and frequently

A firm may be in short term financial equilibrium but unprofitable (hence a candidate for future financial crisis) if

  • it anticipates cash inflows reducing profits (e.g. selling products at discount price)
  • it postpones cash outflows (e.g. investment needed to replace old equipment) reducing future profits
